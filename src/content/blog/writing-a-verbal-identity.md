Visual systems get the attention. Verbal identity is what teams reach for on a Tuesday when they have to write a headline, a product string, or a stage line. If that kit is a page of adjectives — bold, human, refined — it will not survive contact with real work.

A verbal identity should tell someone how to sound, what to avoid, and how to judge a line without calling the studio.

## Start from use, not personality

Ask where the words will appear first. Product empty states, sales decks, and campaign films need different lengths and different levels of claim. Write examples in those formats, not in a vacuum.

A line that works on a homepage can fail in a headset prompt. A line that works on stage can feel heavy in an interface. The identity has to hold across those rooms without copying the same sentence everywhere.

## Replace adjectives with moves

Instead of “confident,” show the move: lead with the product truth, then the benefit. Instead of “warm,” show the move: use plain verbs, keep sentences short, do not add a slogan where a fact will do.

Give the team three to five moves. More than that and nobody remembers them. Pair each move with a weak example and a stronger one. People learn voice by comparison.

## Name the words you will not use

Every category has a pile of default language. Innovative. Seamless. Next generation. If those words are already on every competitor slide, they cannot carry a distinctive brand.

A short “do not use” list is more useful than a long “do use” list. It gives writers a way to edit themselves.

## Make a handbook, not a manifesto

The deliverable should be short enough to open in a meeting. Positioning in one paragraph. Audience in a few lines. Moves, banned words, and a set of sample lines for web, product, and live contexts.

If the handbook needs a workshop to explain it, it is not finished. The point of verbal identity is that the next person in the company can pick it up without rewriting the strategy.

When the words and the visuals follow the same idea, the brand starts to feel inevitable. That is the job. Personality is the byproduct, not the brief.
